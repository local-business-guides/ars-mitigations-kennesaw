What should you do first after a kitchen fire?

Wait until the fire department says it is safe to enter. If there is a chance the fire affected wiring, the ceiling, or other parts of the home, don't assume the area is safe just because the flames are out. Keep children and pets away from soot and debris.

Avoid wiping soot with a damp cloth or running fans through affected rooms before you know what has been deposited. Some soot smears easily, and moving air can spread fine particles to clean areas. If the kitchen or nearby rooms have a strong odor, visible residue, or damaged materials, arrange an assessment before deciding what can be cleaned or kept.

What should smoke damage cleanup involve?

A thorough plan starts with inspecting the kitchen and nearby spaces, not just the wall or appliance closest to the fire. Smoke can move through doorways and ventilation pathways, while heat and suppression water may affect different areas. The provider should explain which surfaces and belongings are affected and how each will be handled.

  • A review of visible soot, smoke odor, heat effects, and any water from firefighting.
  • A written or clearly explained scope that separates cleaning from items or materials that may need removal or repair.
  • Cleaning methods suited to the affected surface, rather than one approach for every material.
  • Odor treatment and a way to check whether the odor remains after cleaning.
  • A plan for protecting unaffected rooms and managing debris.

Ask the provider to explain what is included and what is not. For example, smoke cleanup, contents cleaning, water mitigation, and rebuilding can be separate parts of a project. Confirm who is responsible for each part before work starts.

What will a local professional check in the home?

A local professional should look past the most obvious soot marks. The inspection may include adjoining rooms, ceilings, cabinets, soft furnishings, and areas near vents or door openings. The goal is to understand the full affected area so residue or odor isn't left behind in a space that looked clean at first.

  • Where soot is visible and whether it transfers when a surface is checked.
  • Which materials are porous, delicate, or likely to need a different cleaning approach.
  • Whether smoke odor is present outside the kitchen.
  • Any wet materials or standing water that need attention alongside the smoke cleanup.
  • Whether damaged building materials or electrical components need assessment by the appropriate trade.

Before agreeing to work, ask the inspector to show you the areas they found and explain the proposed sequence. If the scope changes once materials are opened or cleaned, request an explanation and updated approval before additional work proceeds.

What affects the cost of smoke damage cleanup?

There is no reliable price without seeing the affected home. Cost can change with the size of the area, how far smoke traveled, the amount and type of residue, the surfaces involved, odor treatment needs, and whether contents can be cleaned in place or need separate handling.

Fire suppression water can add drying or water restoration work, and damaged cabinets, drywall, flooring, or other materials may require removal and repair. Those tasks may be outside a basic smoke-cleaning scope. Ask for the estimate to identify included work, exclusions, and conditions that could change the total, without relying on a low initial figure that leaves major tasks unspecified.

How long can cleanup take, and what can change the schedule?

Timing depends on the affected area, the amount of cleaning and odor treatment required, and whether water or building repairs are part of the job. A small, contained cleanup may follow a different schedule from a fire that affected several rooms or left materials needing removal.

Ask for a schedule that identifies the assessment, cleaning, drying if needed, and any repair stages. The timeline can shift if hidden damage is found, materials need special handling, or other trades must complete work before the next step. A provider should tell you what could affect the plan and update you when it changes.

What mistakes should you avoid before hiring?

  • Don't assume the cleanup is limited to the kitchen because that is where the fire started.
  • Don't scrub soot or use household cleaners on unknown residue before a professional explains a safe method for the material.
  • Don't choose solely by the lowest estimate if it leaves out odor, contents, water, or repair work you need.
  • Don't approve extra work without understanding why it is needed and how it changes the scope.
  • Don't treat a deodorizer as a substitute for removing or cleaning the source of the odor.

Keep records of the damage and conversations with providers, and check with your insurer about documentation requirements if you plan to make a claim. For safety, follow instructions from the fire department and any qualified professional assessing the property.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • Will you inspect rooms beyond the kitchen for smoke residue and odor?
  • What specific cleaning and odor-treatment work is included in the estimate?
  • How will you handle contents, wet materials, and items that may not be cleanable?
  • Does your scope include water mitigation or repairs, or will another provider be needed?
  • What could change the cost or schedule after work begins, and how will you get my approval?
  • Who will be my day-to-day contact, and how will you report progress?
  • What documentation will you provide for completed work and any recommended follow-up?
  • If repairs are needed, who is responsible for coordinating that work?
